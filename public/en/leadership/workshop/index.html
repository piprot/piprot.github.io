Subsection 03 · Practice as a group

Ascent Leadership Workshop

One scenario, a group of people each moving independently, then a look at the distribution of choices and the reasons behind them. The differences are the teaching material — harder to forget than a lecture. Workshops are organized around team consensus, skills training, and strategy co-creation, progressing by day and ending with a page of consensus you can schedule and verify.

01 / How the session runs

Move first on your own, then see why you differ

If a senior person speaks first, everyone else's real judgment disappears. So the order is fixed: independently first, then public.

1

Agree a shared topic

Pick something the team is genuinely stuck on and reshape it into a scenario everyone can enter. If the topic is too big, shrink it — better to practice one judgment per round.

2

Move independently

One scene, one dilemma, three options. Each person writes their own choice and rationale first — no discussion, no persuasion, no looking at others' answers.

3

Reveal the distribution

Lay out the distribution of choices and rounds, then compare with expert benchmarks. The point is to see where the information we used, the costs we bore, and our expectations of consequences differed — not who was right.

4

Cross-examine

Let people who chose differently explain themselves and surface the assumptions hidden behind their wording. The dissenters often hold the information the organization most needs.

5

Review and transfer

Close with three questions across fact, theory, and transfer layers, then write one page: consensus, remaining disagreements, next actions, and each person's commitments.

A workshop's value is not explaining a theory, but letting people in one room see for the first time exactly where their judgments of the same thing diverge.

02 / Three formats

Choose by topic and group size; no need to run all at once

01 / Scenario workshop

2–8 people sharing a scenario

Good for practicing one judgment per round: a cohort of new managers, a team with competing views on one issue, or surfacing hidden disagreements.

Output:Choice distribution, a list of disagreements, and a one-page consensus.

02 / Two-person practice on one device

Bring 1-on-1 into the classroom

Two people see the same scenario, take turns choosing, then compare against expert benchmarks. Good for short practice rounds in classes and internal training, without needing a device per person.

Output:Rationales and review notes for each round.

03 / Thematic internal training and co-creation

Organized by day

A day built around team consensus, skills training, or strategy co-creation, alternating scenario practice with teaching and ending with decisions on real issues.

Output:Consensus notes and a schedulable change list.

Thematic workshops run by day; travel and venue are agreed separately. The exact format follows team size and topic complexity rather than a fixed schedule.

03 / What remains afterwards

Process evidence is worth more than the room's energy

When a workshop ends, what you can carry back into the organization is material you can keep using — not photos and scores.

Review report

The rationale, consequence, and ability change for each move can be compiled into a text report for your own records and for a coach to follow up in later one-on-one sessions.

Process view

The coaching side can see ability radars, growth curves, and group comparisons to decide what to practice next. It is a teaching basis, not a ranking of participants.

Organizational diagnosis

Look at why problems recur along three lines — process, responsibility, and information — and turn the disagreements exposed in the room into a schedulable, verifiable change list.

Transfer tasks

Give each person a small task in real work: next time a similar situation arises, write down the judgment made at the time and retest after a while.

Ability profiles and scores are used only for feedback and instructional design, not performance evaluation. This is stated at the outset, or people start choosing the option that looks correct.

04 / Boundaries

What fits and what does not

Good fit

A cohort of new managers; a team long divided on the same issue; graduate and high-potential programs needing comparable process evidence; turning one argument into a discussable list of disagreements.

Not a fit

Wanting a one-off lecture; hoping for a single standard answer on the spot; planning to use scores for performance review; expecting one workshop to change behavior.

Four ground rules

· No standard answers: roles and stages differ, so reasonable choices differ; the workshop discusses grounds, not right and wrong.
· No ranking or performance conclusions: live data is used only for teaching and review.
· No instant results: behavior change depends on follow-up transfer tasks and retesting.
· No unverified result figures.
